How it works

The loader cycle

The level has four stopes open at once, one brow for each loader in the largest fleet compared, and each loader bogs from a stope brow. With fewer open brows a spare loader waits for one, which caps the fleet before the operator does. One operator takes remote control at the brow before each fill or hang-up clearing attempt, and keeps control from a successful clear straight into the fill. The operator hands over at the start of the shift and takes one crib break at mid shift, after finishing the current job. Loaders that need the operator wait during both. A loaded machine trams to the dump, empties its bucket and returns on its own. A bucket adds tonnes when it is dumped. A hang-up ties up the operator during clearing. If it needs secondary breaking, which in longhole stopes is usually a secondary blast, a crew goes in to charge the brow. The charge is fired at the next firing window, at crib or at shift end, and the brow reopens after the re-entry time. Until then its loader waits or relocates to another brow. With firing windows off, the brow closes for the fixed block time instead.

The main drive is single lane. Every brow is the same tram distance from the dump, so a nearer junction has a longer crosscut. Loaded machines get first use of a clear segment, followers keep their separation, and opposing machines wait in passing bays at the junctions and the dump. Only one moving loader can be within 20 m of a junction or of the dump tip at a time, so a loader waits short of a busy junction. Waiting for an occupied brow counts as waiting for a brow, not as traffic. A relocating loader drives at normal speed and spends the rest of its relocation time at the new brow. The whole level is one autonomous zone. Real operations often run each loader in its own zone or on another level, which removes traffic waits and means an isolation stops only one loader. Separate zones need separate drives, which this schematic level does not have. Loaders usually pass by tucking into a crosscut rather than a cut bay. Turning traffic off barely changes the operator's limit here.

Auto-stops can occur during autonomous travel, dumping or positioning. The loader stays in place while its reset waits in the operator queue and during the reset itself. The operator can reset it without an open brow. Most auto-stops are remote resets. The share set by Stops needing entry, such as rock on the drive, need someone to go in and isolate the level first. With reset priority on, waiting resets are handled in arrival order before the next fill or duty. The current job finishes first. Turning single lane traffic off selects the v1.1 comparison and switches reset priority off. You can then turn reset priority on separately.

Level isolation

Before anyone enters the level, for a secondary blast or a manual recovery, the operator isolates it. This job goes ahead of fills and resets. Until the isolation ends nothing is filled and no loader moves. Loaders on the move stop where they are and each needs a reset afterwards. A loader part way through a tip or positioning finishes it first. With Secondary blast isolates level off, only the hung-up crosscut is barricaded and the other loaders keep working, which is the closest this level gets to separate zones. Isolation time is left out of working time, so it costs tonnes rather than raising the operator's busy share. If crib falls during an isolation the operator takes it then, and the overlap is counted once.

The operator's limit

A loader limit is the largest fleet that keeps the operator at or below the busy ceiling, measured over working time. If the next loader already adds under 10% to mean tonnes before the ceiling is reached, the limit stops there. Then the cause is either the open brows, when every brow is in use, or little gain. The ceiling is an assumption. Change it under Automation to see how the answer moves.

Calibration

The Kiirunavaara study reports a median loading duration of 50 s, measured from remote takeover to re-activation. With the default inputs, Normal uses a 41 s mean fill with 20% variability plus a 10 s takeover, which gives a modelled median of 50 s over the same start and finish. At the dump the hydraulic tip time comes from the selected loader's specification sheet, and the time to raise and lower the bucket is assumed.

Waiting for the operator

The answer cards show the 90th percentile wait from a loader joining the operator queue until the operator starts its fill or its reset. Waits during handover and crib are included. Time while the level is isolated is left out, because no loader can be served then.

Two exact checks

N is the loader count. F, T and A are the mean fill time, remote takeover time and mean autonomous trip time in seconds. With fixed times the fleet rate is limited by the loader cycles and the operator's filling rate.

Buckets per hour = 3,600 × min(N / (F + T + A), 1 / (F + T))

With exponential times, the machine interference formula gives the probability P₀ that the operator is idle. Set r = F / A and sum over k from 0 to N.

P₀ = 1 / Σ [N! / (N − k)! × rᵏ]
Utilisation = 1 − P₀
Buckets per hour = 3,600 × utilisation / F

With enough brows and takeover, auto-stops, hang-ups, handover and other duties turned off, the simulation matches the formula within 1 percent over 20,000 simulated hours for each fleet size.

Fragmentation uncertainty

Normal loading time is calibrated to measured semi-autonomous loading at a Swedish sublevel caving mine, and the step to Coarse comes from an open pit study, so both are starting points, not WA stope data. No published hang-up rate for WA longhole stope brows was found, so hang-ups stay assumed. The share of hang-ups that need a secondary blast matters most. With the default inputs, coarse muck reaches the busy ceiling at three loaders when that share is 10%, but stays under it when the share is 20% or more. More secondary blasts close more brows and isolate the level for longer, so loaders wait and ask the operator for less, and the cost shows up as lost tonnes instead.

Across 169 bench blasts, the median size estimate was off by about −30% on average. The middle half of errors extended from roughly −75% to +50%. Sanchidrián and Ouchterlony (2017)

Operating limits

Tonnes are a ceiling with no breakdowns. The model leaves out trucks, a full stockpile, breakdowns, ventilation and re-entry after production blasts. Each stope also never runs out of broken rock during the shift.
